# Drafting

What is held in mind while a scene is written, and the failure modes that appear when it isn't. Entry format is described in `../README.md`.

---

### The chapter kernel

**What.** One sentence, found before drafting, naming **what the people in the scene are doing to each other** — carried into the draft instead of the notes.

**When.** Before drafting any chapter or scene. Re-found if the scene is rewritten.

**How.** **Derive it from the strongest clash between a motive and an obstacle that is closest to the story's arc** (mikel, September 22 2026), then decorate it with subplots harvested from what is going on around the main movement. The procedure: list every character in the scene with motive, goal, and obstacle; find the pair of motives the situation has set against each other; check which clash the book's arc runs through; that is the kernel, and the other clashes are the subplots. Kernels proposed from a character's manner or a running gag ("everyone treats him as the junior") are decoration mistaken for the center. The sentence names both people and the tension between them. A kernel with one person in it is a *situation*; what makes it a *scene* is that the two halves pull against each other — what one is trying to do for the other is being undermined by what is happening to them. The Kestrel example, for the platform chapter: *Jaemon is staging a homecoming for a friend who has never been here, while his own body makes a fool of him.*

Two rules about its use:

- **It replaces the notes in the drafting session.** A keep/cut list, a review queue, or a beat's freight inventory read while writing collapses immersion into analysis, which is the thing the review process exists to prevent (see *Writing and editing are separate modes*, `prose-review.md`). Everything on those lists either happens on its own once the kernel is running, or turns out not to have mattered.
- **It is not available early.** The kernel can only be stated once two things are settled: what each person wants from the other in this scene, and what the chapter is *for*. Asking what a chapter should *contain* before those are settled produces inventories, not kernels — and inventories do not survive contact with drafting.

**Origin.** Kestrel Book 4, September 12 2026 — the chapter 1 re-read; the derivation rule September 22 2026, chapter 7's prep, after three proposed kernels built on manner rather than on the arc's clash were rejected. The practice is the author's recognition of how the chapter's working passages had been written in the first place ("put myself in the scene and write what I experience with the goal in mind"); the sentence itself was arrived at only after the author corrected a wrong read of the character's behavior, which is the general case.

**Scope.** General.

**See also.** *The world enters through the transaction*, below; *Writing and editing are separate modes*, `prose-review.md`.

### The world enters through the transaction

**What.** Setting, mechanism, and situation are delivered through whatever is passing between the characters at that moment — not beside it, and not instead of it. A stretch with no transaction in it cannot carry the world, and the material will read as exposition however it is phrased.

**When.** Any scene that has to establish a place, a technology, or a state of affairs. Acute in science fiction and fantasy, where the environment is invisible unless it is given to the reader.

**How.** The working question at any point in a scene is not *how do I convey this place* but **what are these two doing to each other right now, and what does this place give them to do it with.** Whatever they are in the middle of is the only channel the setting can use: if they are arguing, the setting is evidence in the argument; if one is complaining, the setting is what he is complaining about; if one is being kind, the setting is what the kindness is about.

The diagnostic follows from it. When a passage of world-delivery reads flat, the defect is usually not in the delivery — it is that the passage has no transaction to attach to, so the material attaches to nothing and sits there. **The setting problem is downstream of the moment problem, and dissolves when the moment is supplied.** Installing world into a stretch with nothing happening in it fails at any level of craft, which is why such stretches survive repeated revision unimproved.

Two corollaries worth holding:

- **Appearance carries scale only for objects the reader already knows the size of.** A known object's apparent size gives its distance; an unknown object's gives nothing, and no visual construction supplies the missing number. One of the two figures must simply be stated — and stated in the units the characters would use (people, days of travel), not the units the reference layer keeps (kilometres, diameters).
- **Statement is not the sin; accumulation is.** Exposition goes clunky at paragraph scale, not at clause scale. A few sentences of straight assertion — most naturally at a scene's opening, before anything is in motion — are within budget and are rarely skipped. It is the fifth paragraph that readers leave.

**Origin.** Kestrel Book 4, September 12 2026 — derived during the chapter 1 re-read from the author's own working passages, in which gravity arrives through a man's humiliation, a technology through his complaint to his physician, and a cargo's contents through a disagreement about whether the thing in the box is a person.

**Scope.** Fiction; acute in genre with an unfamiliar setting.

**See also.** *Ventriloquized exposition*, below; *The chapter kernel*, above.

### Ventriloquized exposition

**What.** Converting a passage of exposition into dialogue does not remove the exposition. It puts quotation marks on it. The named failure mode: **exposition in a dialogue costume.**

**When.** Whenever a review has flagged exposition and the remedy is to give the material to characters to say.

**How.** The test is not whether a line has quotation marks but what the line is *doing*. A character asking a question whose only function is to prompt an answer the reader needs, and a character supplying facts the other character already knows, are exposition regardless of form — a slide projector with a friend's voice attached. The elliptical-dialogue rule (Leonard: leave out the parts readers skip; readers skip exposition to get to dialogue) condemns these exchanges *more* firmly than it condemns narration, and pointing the rule at form rather than content is what allows such a stretch to survive several revisions while getting steadily more dialogue-shaped and no less inert.

Distinguish from narration that is not exposition: narration reporting what people *do*, and narration carrying judgment, joke, or withholding, are not what the rule cuts. The test for a paragraph of narration is whether cutting it loses the reader a **fact** or a **laugh**. A fact, cut it; a laugh, it stays.

**Origin.** Kestrel Book 4, September 12 2026 — diagnosed on the chapter 1 walk-to-the-station, which had twice been revised from narration into dialogue and twice failed to improve.

**Scope.** Fiction.

**See also.** *The world enters through the transaction*, above.

### A fact belongs where someone has a reason to deliver it

**What.** A piece of world reaches the reader through whoever has a reason to say it. There are two hosts — a **scene in which the fact is the situation**, and a **person for whom telling it is characterization** — and forcing a fact into a passage that is neither is most of what makes writing read as exposition.

**When.** Whenever something about the setting has to reach the reader and the obvious place for it is a digression.

**How.** Take the hosts in order of how often they are overlooked.

- **The fact is the situation.** The scene is *about* the thing, so nothing is explained. Weight draining out of a body over a two-day climb is a lecture in a conversation on a dock; in a car on the cable it is merely what is happening.
- **Telling it is characterization.** Frequently no such scene exists — most often because the POV character is not present for it. Then the fact can be carried by someone for whom *saying it* reveals who they are, and the register does the work the scene would have done: the same content out of a young crewman still lit up from the ride is not instruction, it is a person. This host is cheaper than the scene, survives POV constraints that kill the scene, and is routinely missed.
- **Deferral is a real answer.** When neither host exists where the fact is wanted, the answer is usually *not here* rather than *how do I phrase it*. A fact forced early is the commonest cause of a passage that no revision improves.

**The test, and the trap.** Feeling in the voice is not the test. A fact converted into a complaint, a joke, or a grievance is still exposition **if the stretch has stopped being about the people and started being about the mechanism.** Ask what the passage is now *about*, not whether the information is in someone's mouth. (This is *Ventriloquized exposition* at one level up, and it catches the more plausible version of the same mistake.)

**Origin.** Kestrel Book 4, September 12 2026. Arrived at through three wrong attempts at one fact — proposed as narration (exposition), then as a character's complaint (a grievance whose content is a mechanism is still a lecture), then as a scene (impossible; the narrator is not aboard). The project's own earlier ruling had already chosen the second host: the fact was given to an eager young crewman to report on arrival, where it reads as enthusiasm rather than instruction.

**Scope.** Fiction.

**See also.** *The world enters through the transaction* and *Ventriloquized exposition*, above; *Narrator tracking and the presence audit*, `outline-and-beats.md`.

### A repetition that performs a different speech act is not a repetition

**What.** A fact stated twice is not necessarily said twice. When the second instance is a **different speech act** — consolation, reassurance, emphasis, a concession, closing something off — it is new material, and cutting it as redundant removes the act while keeping the information.

**When.** Any time a review, a line edit, or an efficiency instinct flags a restatement.

**How.** Ask what the second instance is *doing*, not what it says. Narration establishing a fact and a character offering that same fact to a friend who is miserable are not the same utterance: the first informs the reader, the second is comfort, and "only two more days" said to someone having a bad time is not a schedule. That is also how people actually talk — restating what everyone present already knows is the ordinary way of performing an act that isn't informational.

The practical half is usually decisive as well. Replacing the restatement with a reference to it ("we'll be done soon") is generally *more* expensive, because it obliges the prose to re-establish why — so the apparent economy costs words and loses the act.

**The pair.** This is the mirror of *Ventriloquized exposition* and of the trap in *A fact belongs where someone has a reason to deliver it*. There, the surface looks characterful and is exposition. Here, the surface looks redundant and is character. **Informational economy is the wrong test in both directions; what the line is doing is the test.**

**Origin.** Kestrel Book 4, September 12 2026 — mikel's ruling on a chapter 1 note. A journey's duration was stated twice, once in narration and once in dialogue, and was flagged as a redundancy creating reader confusion. The author's ruling: the second instance is the narrator consoling his friend, and the restatement is what makes the consolation legible without a further explanation. The note was withdrawn as a wrong diagnosis rather than deferred.

**Scope.** General.

**See also.** *Ventriloquized exposition* and *A fact belongs where someone has a reason to deliver it*, above.

### A running order is written from, so every step is self-contained

**What.** The running order in a chapter plan is the document the author drafts from, start to finish, without leaving it. Every step states **what happens, who does it, and — wherever it bears on the step — why** (motive and context), in full sentences, succinctly. No step refers the writer elsewhere for its own content.

**When.** Writing or amending a chapter plan's running order, or any list the author will draft from. Also whenever a running order is *updated*: rulings folded in as pointers ("see the boxed statement," "per the MOTIVES section") are the usual way this rule gets broken.

**How.** Each step answers, inline: what happens; who; why, if motive or context matters to writing it; and any guard (what must not happen, and why). **Forbidden inside a step:** a pointer in place of content — "see above," "trove S2," "the splinter," "topics 1 and 2," a ruling's date without the ruling's substance. Provenance may stay as a brief bracket *after* the content. If the content lives elsewhere in the plan, copy it into the step: duplication inside an ephemeral plan costs nothing, and a lookup costs immersion. The rest of the plan — kernel, step-by-step, provenance — may be as cross-referenced as it likes; the running order may not. **The test:** read the running order alone with the rest of the file covered; if any step makes you want to look something up, it fails.

**Origin.** Kestrel Book 4, September 19 2026 — mikel, after the chapter 6 running order had been brought up to date with a day's rulings by pointers. His words: "many of the steps in the running order are too elliptical to write from… when I'm writing I have to break immersion to look up what is meant. Each step should say exactly what happens and, when motivation and context are important to the step, they should be given explicitly (but succinctly)." He noted it was not the first time he had asked, and made it a rule.

**Scope.** General to this collaboration.

**See also.** *The chapter kernel*, above; *Beat paragraphs are compressions, not stage directions*, `outline-and-beats.md` (the outline's register is the opposite one — compressed — and that is correct there; the running order is where compression stops).
